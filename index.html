Home Ownership: Is the Australian Dream Real?

How age, income, household circumstances and place shape home ownership across Australia.

Australia Still Owns, But the Balance Is Shifting

Home ownership remains the majority tenure, but the way Australians own and rent has changed.

Ownership still dominates, but only when its two forms are combined.
66.9% of dwellings are owner-occupied, split almost evenly between mortgages (35.5%) and outright ownership (31.4%). Renting accounts for another 31.1%.

The largest shift is within ownership itself.
Outright ownership fell from 41.8% to 29.5%, while mortgaged households rose from 29.6% to 36.8%. Renting also increased, from 25.7% to 31.4%.

Australia has not stopped being a nation of homeowners, but ownership has changed. Fewer households own outright, more remain tied to a mortgage, and renting occupies a larger share than it did 25 years ago.

But national averages hide who has experienced the biggest change. Age reveals the first major divide.

The Ownership Divide Starts With Age

Older Australians are far more likely to own, and younger generations are reaching the same ages with lower ownership rates.

The tenure balance flips during the 30s.
Only 40.7% of households headed by someone aged 25-34 own their home, compared with 56.7% among 35-44 year olds. Ownership then continues rising with age.

Age alone does not explain the gap.
Among 30-34 year olds, ownership falls from 68.0% for the earliest observed cohort to 49.7% for the latest. This is an 18.3 percentage-point gap at the same stage of life.

The decline becomes smaller with age.
Between the earliest observable Census and 2021, ownership fell about 18 percentage points for Australians aged 25-34, but only 3 points for those aged 60-64.

Younger Australians are not simply waiting longer to reach the ownership rates of earlier generations, they are reaching the same ages with substantially lower ownership.

Is that uniquely Australian? Comparing similar countries shows that the generational shift extends beyond Australia, but not everywhere in the same direction.

Australia isn't alone

Australia records the second-largest decline in this comparison.
Ownership among people in their 30s fell 15 percentage points, behind the UK's 18-point fall. Canada remained almost unchanged, while the Netherlands increased by 9 points.

Falling ownership among younger adults is therefore not an inevitable feature of wealthy economies. Australia's experience is substantial, but international differences suggest that age alone cannot explain it.
Within Australia, household structure and income reveal another layer of the divide.

Income and Household Structure Reshape the Odds

Who you live with matters, but income often changes the tenure picture just as dramatically.

Renting is concentrated among some household structures.
Group households are overwhelmingly renters, while couples with children flow predominantly towards mortgages. Couples without children are much more strongly represented among outright owners.

Household type is only part of the story.
Among couples with children, ownership rises from 53.9% below $800 per week to 86.4% above $3,500. Across all incomes, group households have the lowet overall ownership rate at just 29.7%.

The tenure ranking flips as income rises.
Mortgages rank third among households below $800 per week, but become the most common tenure from $1,500-2,499 onward and reach 58.1% among households above $3,500.

Income does not guarantee ownership, but it strongly changes the pathway. Higher-income households are far more likely to be paying off a home, while renting dominates some lower-income and shared household arrangements.
Yet Australians with similar household circumstances still encounter very different housing markets depending on where they live.

Where You Live Changes the Odds

Ownership and renting are unevenly distributed across Australia, especially within the major metro areas.

The national ownership rate hides strong local variation.
Many surburban and regional SA2s record high ownership, while lower rates cluster in inner-city, remote and specialised housing areas.

Greater Melbourne is not one housing market.
Ownership varies from below 20% in some SA2s to above 90% in others, showing that metropolitan averages conceal sharply different local tenure patterns.
Ownership appears to increase in areas as it moves away from the inner-city areas.

High renting does not necessarily mean cheaper renting.
Some inner-Sydney SA2s combine renter shares above 70% with median weekly rents above $500, placing both tenure pressure and rent cost in the same locations.
The common trend appears to be that inner-city areas have many renters that pay high renting costs.

Large renter populations concentrate around inner metropolitan areas.
Melbourne CBD-North records nearly 6,900 rented dwellings, with other major concentrations around inner Melbourne, Sydney and Brisbane.

The maps reveal two different kinds of concentration: some places have a high share of renters, while others contain a large number of renter households. Neither pattern is visible from state averages alone. To see how much those averages conceal, the final analysis moves below the state level.
